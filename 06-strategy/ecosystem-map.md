## Threat Map

| Threat | Likelihood | Potential impact | Counter-strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creators remain on Instagram/DMs | High | Musa fails to become habitual | Complement social sharing; make Musa the structured link destination |
| Marketplace lacks enough supply | **High** | Buyers see an empty or repetitive feed | Seed one geography and several categories; onboard creators directly |
| Low buyer trust or fraudulent listings | Medium | Weak conversion and reputational harm | Reporting, creator verification, clear policies, reviews after verified interactions |
| Etsy, MyMarket, or another platform improves local fit | Medium | Differentiation narrows | Build local relationships, data, catalogue density, and Georgian workflows |
| A funded local copycat enters | Medium | Faster feature development and marketing | Focus on community presence, creator retention, and exclusive learning loops |
| Payments or delivery are operationally difficult | Medium–High | Interest does not become completed sales | Pilot inquiry flow first; partner rather than build logistics prematurely |
| Team capacity falls after the course | High | Product becomes unmaintained | Assign ownership, document operations, limit scope, set continuation decision date |
| Dependency on infrastructure vendors | Low–Medium | Outage, pricing, or policy changes affect Musa | Backups, portable schema, environment documentation, export procedures |
| Privacy or security incident | Medium | User harm and loss of trust | Least-data design, access control, breach process, retention policy, security reviews |

## Strategic Priorities

Musa’s first ecosystem priority is marketplace liquidity: recruit enough active creators and listings in a narrow Georgian handmade segment before expanding features or geography. The second priority is conversion infrastructure. A lightweight inquiry or order-request flow should connect discovery to measurable buyer intent without forcing an early, expensive payment integration. Third, Musa should formalise distribution partnerships with craft fairs, university entrepreneurship groups, and creator micro-communities; these partners can seed trusted supply more efficiently than broad paid advertising. Fourth, the team should prepare—but not yet overbuild—local payment and delivery integrations by interviewing providers and mapping operational requirements. Fifth, Musa must reduce platform dependency through database backups, documented deployment, and portable data models. Every partnership should support one of three metrics: activated creators, live listings, or qualified buyer actions. During the next 60 days, the team should avoid partnerships that add visibility but do not produce measurable marketplace activity. The ecosystem strategy is therefore focused, local, and evidence-led rather than based on collecting logos.
