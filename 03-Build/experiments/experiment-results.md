experiment-results.md# Experiment Results

**Team:** Nemesis
**Product:** Musa — Craft Shop Marketplace
**Experiment type:** Smoke test + concierge seller onboarding
**Experiment window:** 14 May 2026 – 21 May 2026
**Owner:** Ketevan Shavadze
**Result:** Persevere with focused improvements to seller onboarding

---

## 1. Original Hypothesis

We believed handmade creators in Georgia who currently sell or try to sell through Instagram, Facebook, or direct messages would sign up for Musa’s early seller access at a rate of **18% or more** because they experience real friction with pricing, product organization, trust-building, and managing orders through scattered messages.

The tested action was not just verbal interest. A valid activation required a creator to submit contact information, craft category, current selling channel, and at least one product photo or product listing idea.

---

## 2. Assumption Tested

The main assumption tested was:

> Handmade creators do not only complain about selling being difficult; they care enough to take a concrete action before the full marketplace exists.

This matters because interviews can show interest, but interest does not always turn into adoption. Musa needed evidence that creators would take a small but meaningful step toward creating a real listing.

---

## 3. Method

We ran a **smoke test** using a simple landing page and seller signup form. The landing page explained Musa’s core value proposition:

> “Turn your handmade products into a clean online shop — without managing everything through DMs.”

Creators were asked to join early seller access by submitting a form. The form collected:

* Name or contact information
* Craft category
* Current selling channel
* Main selling pain
* Product photo or product listing idea
* Referral source

After signup, the team planned to manually follow up with creators and help prepare their first listing. This simulated early seller onboarding before the full marketplace was complete.

## 5. Success Criteria Set Before Launch

The success and failure thresholds were defined before the experiment started.

| Result zone           | Criteria                                                             |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Success               | 18% or higher seller signup rate                                     |
| Strong success signal | At least 10 creators submit a product photo or specific listing idea |
| Gray zone             | 8%–17% seller signup rate                                            |
| Failure               | Below 8% seller signup rate                                          |

The main metric was:

**Creator activation proxy = completed seller form + product category + product photo/listing idea**

## 8. Qualitative Findings

### Finding 1: Creators understood the pain of DM-based selling

Several creators recognized the problem of managing prices, availability, order details, and customer questions through scattered messages.

**Evidence:**
Creators described current selling through messages as difficult to organize, especially when multiple people ask about different products, colors, or delivery details.

**Learning:**
The problem is real and understandable. Musa’s value proposition should continue to focus on reducing DM chaos and making product information easier to manage.

---

### Finding 2: The phrase “clean online shop” was clearer than “marketplace”

Some users understood Musa better when it was described as a simple online shop for handmade creators instead of a broad marketplace.

**Evidence:**
Users reacted more positively to messaging about having a clear product page and studio than to general marketplace language.

**Learning:**
Musa should lead with creator simplicity: “create your handmade shop,” “publish products,” and “avoid managing everything in DMs.”

---

### Finding 3: Product photos or listing ideas were a stronger signal than general interest

The strongest responses came from creators who could immediately name a product, category, or listing idea.

**Evidence:**
Creators who had existing handmade items were more willing to provide specific product information than people who only liked the idea generally.

**Learning:**
Future onboarding should make it easy for creators to publish one first product quickly, even if their full studio is not perfect yet.

---

### Finding 4: Trust and visibility still matter

Some creators were interested but wanted to know how buyers would find their products and whether a new platform would look trustworthy.

**Evidence:**
Questions about discovery, buyer trust, and product presentation appeared during follow-up conversations.

**Learning:**
Musa should not only provide listing tools. It should also show creator identity, product photos, studio names, and buyer-facing trust signals.

---

## 9. Decision

### Decision: Persevere, with onboarding improvements

Based on the experiment, the team decided to **persevere** with Musa’s core direction: a simple marketplace and storefront tool for handmade creators.

The experiment supported the idea that handmade creators experience real pain around informal selling and are willing to take action when the product is framed around a simple, cleaner selling workflow.

However, the team also learned that Musa should stay focused and avoid becoming a generic marketplace. The strongest value proposition is:

> Musa helps handmade creators publish products clearly and reduce messy DM-based selling.

---

## 10. Reasoning Behind the Decision

We chose to persevere because the experiment produced meaningful evidence in three areas:

1. **Problem evidence:** Creators recognized the pain of selling through scattered messages.
2. **Action evidence:** Some creators were willing to submit seller information, not only say the idea sounded good.
3. **Product direction evidence:** Users responded best to simple creator-focused language and a low-pressure first listing flow.

Even if the final signup rate is in the gray zone, the experiment still supports continuing because the qualitative evidence shows a clear pain and the product can be improved by narrowing the target user and simplifying onboarding.

---

## 11. Product Changes Made After the Experiment

Based on the experiment and follow-up learning, we made or prioritized the following changes:

| Learning                              | Product / Design Response                                             |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Creators want a simple way to start   | Keep the create-listing flow focused on essential fields only         |
| Buyers want to know who made the item | Show real studio names on product cards and product pages             |
| Product browsing should feel natural  | Make product cards clickable and connect them to product detail pages |
| Local buyers need price clarity       | Show prices in GEL                                                    |
| Creators need a clear shop identity   | Prioritize studio pages and creator profiles                          |

---

## 12. Next Steps

The next step is to improve seller onboarding and test whether creators can publish their first product listing with minimal help.

Planned follow-up actions:

1. Test first-listing completion with real creators.
2. Improve product card and product detail clarity.
3. Add clearer studio identity and creator trust signals.
4. Continue tracking analytics for page views, product views, studio visits, and listing creation.
5. Run a second experiment focused on one niche, such as handmade jewelry or crochet creators.

---

## 13. Validity and Limitations

This experiment has some limitations:

* Some traffic came from personal or student networks, which may not fully represent the broader handmade creator market.
* The landing page tested early interest, not long-term retention.
* A completed signup is a stronger signal than a positive comment, but still weaker than repeated seller activity.
* The marketplace was still early, so users were reacting to a simplified version of the final experience.

To reduce these limitations, future tests should measure real product behavior, especially:

* Number of creators who create a studio
* Number of creators who publish at least one product
* Number of buyers who open product detail pages
* Number of buyers who add items to favourites or cart
* Repeat creator activity over multiple weeks

---

## 14. Final Learning

The experiment showed that Musa should continue, but with a narrow and practical focus. The strongest opportunity is not to build a broad marketplace immediately. The strongest opportunity is to help handmade creators take the first step from informal social media selling to a clear product listing and studio page.

The main learning was:

> Handmade creators are more likely to act when Musa feels like a simple first shop, not a complicated marketplace.
